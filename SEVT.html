Solstice Electric Vehicle Rear Suspension

As subteam lead, I designed and conducted materials testing for a trailing suspension arm for a solar-powered electric vehicle named Solstice.

Solstice is a 2-seater solar electric vehicle designed by the MIT Solar Electric Vehicle Team for the American Solar Challenge. For the competition, the car must be road-legal and must drive ~300 miles on a track and ~2000 miles across the continental US.

Ideation

My subteam and I started the design process in September 2024 by considering two possible arm designs: an L-shaped arm and a Lambda-shaped arm. The Lambda-Arm was the design used for the team's previous car, and while it kept stresses internal to the system low it caused very high reaction forces into the car chassis (12,000 N for a 4,000 N load). Our goal was to minimize the force transmitted into the chassis of the car, and this was the driving factor in our choice of arm geometry. We used MATLAB to keep track of our calculatons, and I volunteered to do most of the MATLAB work because I had a lot of previous experience with it. After considering constraints generated by other aspects of the car (wheelbase, chassis layout, etc.), we determined that the L-shaped arm would be the better choice.

Refinement

After the preliminary math was complete, the next pressing concern became material. The options were narrowed down to welded steel tubing or machined aluminum. As a team, we do not have the ability to weld aluminum and are limited by the range of travel on our CNC machines. Aluminum would result in a lighter system overall, we would have to split the arm in multiple parts for manufacturing and that would force us to rely on bolted connections. Exploratory designs into aluminum we could not reasonably design a manufacturable arm that met our standards for bolted connections and maintained a weight advantage over steel. In parallel, the designs for a welded steel arm showed comparative promise in manufacturability and a negligible change in system weight compared to the most developed aluminum design that we considered.

At this point in the year, November 2024, I decided to work on the steel arm design, while another team member designed the aluminum version. I believe that my design for the arm was a leading reason for my selection as subteam lead the following semester.

Finalized Design

The spring and summer of 2025 were spent performing FEA and modifying the design of the arm until the team's goal safety factors were met. As of December 2025, the rear suspension has a design that passes FEA under current assumptions and is beginning manufacturing. The FEA was performed on a modified version of the arm assembly, with all welded components of the swing arm merged as one part with fillets applied to model weld beads. Currently, the arm's worst-case safety factor is ~1.25 in the tipping condition for the car in reference to our unwelded mild steel, which I am still working on increasing. However, given how (ideally) rare this loading case is, the goal safety factor is lower than that for more common loading cases (i.e. 1.5 vs. 2 for regulation loading).

According to FEA results for the arm, the design met the team's design goals with flying colors. The maximum reaction force magnitude transmitted into the chassis through any individual mount was reduced 33% compared to our previous car's worst case (8,000 N vs. 12,000 N). This allowed the chassis subteam to design a lighter chassis, because they no longer needed as much reinforcement for the rear suspension mounting panels.

We have performed materials tests in January 2025 using the steel tubes we planned to use on the car, which I used to determine the anticipated strength of welds my subteam will produce. The resultant yield strength and Young's modulus of the welds were determined by matching simulation results to experimentally derived Force-Displacent data.

Manufacturing

Now that the team has begun manufacturing, my work as a part of the team has broadened to helping out any other subteam that needs assistance. With my help, the team has made significant progress towards finishing the chassis of the car. This was also an opporunity to ensure that the rear suspension mounting points and fins are within acceptable tolerance.

Reflection

Looking back, I believe that the design for the suspension came out as well as it could have given our choice of material and the results of our materials testing. My design passed all of our design goals, but I feel that I could have done all that and had a lighter system overall if I decided on aluminum as the material of choice. At the time, steel was a reasonable decision to make, and now there is not enough time to re-design the system from scratch, but I will definitely advocate for a single-piece aluminum arm for our next car.
