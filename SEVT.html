Solstice Electric Vehicle Rear Suspension

As subteam lead, I designed and conducted materials testing for a trailing suspension arm for a solar-powered electric vehicle named Solstice.

Solstice is a 2-seater solar electric vehicle designed by the MIT Solar Electric Vehicle Team for the American Solar Challenge. For the competition, the car must be road-legal and must drive ~300 miles on a track and ~2000 miles across the continental US.

Ideation

My subteam and I started the design process by considering two possible arm designs: an L-shaped arm and a Lambda-shaped arm. The Lambda-Arm was the design used for the team's previous car, and our goal was to minimize the force transmitted into the chassis of the car. We used MATLAB to keep track of our calculatons, and to prevent re-solving everything by hand if we needed to change anything.

Refinement

As a team, we came to the conclusion that the L-Arm design would give us better performance with a smaller footprint, and the next pressing concern became material. The options were narrowed down to welded steel tubing or machined aluminum. Once we had a design for each material, we would choose one based on a design review with other (and former) team members. We also created a 'bounding blob' for the arm to help other subteams design around our arm and help us avoid interference with other subsystems.

I was tasked with designing the steel arm, while another team member designed the aluminum version. In the end, we went with my arm design for ease of manufacturing and because it is welded rather than bolted together.

Finalized Design

As of November 2025, the rear suspension subteam has a design that passes FEA under current assumptions and is ready to begin manufacturing. The FEA was performed on a merged version of the arm, with all welded components of the swing arm merged as one part with fillets applied to model weld beads. Currently, our worst-case safety factor is ~1.25 in the tipping condition for the car in reference to our unwelded mild steel, which we are still working on increasing. However, given how (ideally) rare this loading case will occur, the goal safety factor is lower than that for more common loading cases (i.e. 1.5 vs. 2).

We have performed materials tests (via INSTRON) using the steel tubes we intend to use on the car, which we we used to determine the anticipated strength of welds our team will produce. The resultant yield strength and Young's modulus of the welds were determined by matching simulation results to experimentally derived Force-Displacent data. We are narrowing down our options for off-the-shelf shocks and springs, and aiming to have the entire subsystem assembled by the end of January 2026.
